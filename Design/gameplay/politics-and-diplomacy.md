# Politics and Diplomacy

## Role in the Game

Politics provides alternatives to conquest and creates relationships that can become strategic advantages or liabilities.

Diplomacy should not be a separate menu of attitude modifiers. Factions should have recognizable incentives, capabilities, fears, and opportunities. Discovery reveals what they actually want, allowing the player to make better offers and avoid expensive mistakes.

## Faction Types

The universe may contain:

- Player empires.
- Neighboring hostile aliens.
- Friendly alien species.
- Neutral trade powers.
- Research or cultural factions.
- Rival empires.
- Ancient custodians.
- Compromised or infiltrated allies.
- Invasion-aligned factions.

Each faction should have:

- Territory and strategic position.
- Resources and production capabilities.
- Military strength.
- Diplomatic goals.
- Cultural or species identity.
- Trust and memory.
- Internal pressures.
- Relationships with other factions.
- A threshold for cooperation, neutrality, or hostility.

## Minor Factions

Many neighboring factions should function as minor factions rather than full 4X empires. They have a fixed territory and a distinct strategic value, but do not continuously expand or develop into major powers.

Minor factions can range from single-system enclaves to advanced regional powers controlling six to eight systems or more. Larger minor factions should generally have stronger technology, fleets, and rewards, and tend to appear farther from the player's starting position. These are generation tendencies rather than fixed map rules.

A minor faction may provide:

- A unique resource or production site.
- A faction keyword or specialized technology.
- A rare shipyard, station, or defensive system.
- Trade, access, intelligence, or limited fleet support.
- A buffer or staging position against local threats.

The player can:

- Ignore the faction and route around its territory.
- Trade with it for a modest benefit.
- Protect or support it for stronger access.
- Use diplomacy to unlock its specialty.
- Conquer it for full control of its systems and resources.

Minor factions may be defensive or aggressive:

- **Defensive factions** remain inside their territory and respond when the player settles, mines, builds, or stations fleets near their borders.
- **Aggressive factions** remain within their starting territory but launch sorties when the player approaches or threatens their interests.

Minor factions should be capable of defending themselves and creating local pressure, but they should not expand into major empires, continuously scale their military, or develop enough to defeat the invasion. The invasion should eventually overwhelm an unprotected minor faction.

Protecting a minor faction can delay its defeat and preserve its benefits, but it should cost the player fleets, time, or resources. The faction's purpose is to provide strategic leverage, shortcuts, and opportunity costs—not to become an alternate solution to the campaign's central threat.

## Relationship Model

Relationships should combine visible state with hidden or discoverable context.

Visible factors may include:

- Trust.
- Opinion.
- Threat perception.
- Trade value.
- Treaty status.
- Recent actions.
- Shared enemies.

Hidden or partially known factors may include:

- True strategic goals.
- Faction leadership conflicts.
- Resource shortages.
- Cultural taboos.
- Secret agreements.
- Infiltration.
- Conditions that trigger betrayal or cooperation.

The player should be able to improve relationships through deliberate action, but a high opinion number should not guarantee predictable behavior if the faction's deeper incentives are ignored.

## Diplomatic Actions

Possible actions include:

- Establish contact.
- Share intelligence.
- Trade resources.
- Request access or passage.
- Sign non-aggression agreements.
- Form defensive pacts.
- Request fleet support.
- Exchange technology.
- Offer territory or autonomy.
- Threaten or coerce.
- Conduct espionage.
- Expose a betrayal or infiltration.
- Declare war.

Each action should have a strategic purpose. Trade can solve resource shortages, access can open a route, and a defensive pact can remove the need to garrison a frontier.

## Language and Cultural Discovery

Alien language, anthropology, and cultural research should unlock better diplomatic options.

Possible effects include:

- Understanding faction requests.
- Revealing hidden incentives.
- Opening negotiations with an otherwise inaccessible species.
- Reducing the cost of treaties.
- Avoiding accidental insults or hostile actions.
- Enabling joint fleets or shared technology.
- Identifying which factions can be trusted.

Language knowledge is not an automatic alliance. The player still needs to offer something valuable and protect the relationship.

## Alliances and Betrayal

Alliances should be useful but not unconditional.

An ally may:

- Contribute fleets.
- Protect a border.
- Share a resource.
- Provide access to a route.
- Offer research or cultural knowledge.
- Join the final offensive.

An ally may also:

- Demand support at a bad time.
- Leak information.
- Become infiltrated.
- Flip when threatened.
- Abandon a treaty if its survival requires it.

The player should be able to discover and prepare for these risks. Betrayal should be a learnable strategic problem, not a random event that invalidates a successful plan without warning.

## War and Conquest

Military conflict with non-invasion factions should have objectives beyond destroying every ship.

Possible war goals include:

- Claiming a resource.
- Securing a route.
- Removing a hostile base.
- Forcing access.
- Rescuing a population.
- Capturing a shipyard.
- Breaking an alliance.
- Preventing a faction from aiding the invasion.

Conquest should have costs:

- Fleet losses.
- Occupation or stability problems.
- Diplomatic backlash.
- Resource and infrastructure damage.
- Delay that allows the invasion to advance.

Diplomacy should often be the cheaper solution, but not always the safer or more reliable one.

Internal empire pressure should remain an aggregate colony-stability system defined in [economy-and-building.md](economy-and-building.md), not a separate internal-politics simulation.

## Trade, Access, and Conquest

Trade and conquest should provide different time horizons for solving a resource or territory problem.

**Conquest** provides stronger long-term control:

- Full access to the faction's systems, buildings, and resource output.
- Direct control of routes, shipyards, and strategic positions.
- Freedom from dependence on the faction's continued cooperation.

Conquest also requires a significant short-term commitment:

- Fleet losses, repairs, and supply.
- Occupation and stability costs.
- Diplomatic backlash or coalition formation.
- Time that could have been spent preparing for the invasion.
- Possible damage to the captured infrastructure.

**Trade** provides a lower-risk but weaker alternative:

- A smaller, steady stream of resources or access.
- No need to defeat or occupy the faction.
- Faster access to a needed resource during a critical timeline.
- An opportunity to preserve fleets for the invasion.

Trade should not provide the same long-term output or control as conquest. It can also be disrupted by war, blockades, faction betrayal, or the loss of a route. Its value is that it avoids the immediate cost of war and may be the correct choice while the player is still preparing.

The intended decision is:

> Trade to stabilize or accelerate the current plan; conquer when the long-term value justifies the war.

A player should usually be capable of defeating a neighboring faction after gaining enough timeline power, but the faction's technology, defenses, and strategic location should make the short-term cost meaningful. Diplomatic play remains worthwhile when the invasion deadline makes conquest inefficient or when the faction provides additional fleet, route, or technology benefits.

## Expansion Strain and Integration

Expansion should create a soft, self-balancing cost rather than a hard empire limit.

Owning more star systems can increase empire-wide logistical and administrative strain:

- Higher global upkeep.
- More route and relay maintenance.
- More systems requiring defense and sensor coverage.
- Greater fleet redeployment demands.
- More exposure to raids and sabotage.
- Higher costs for sustaining distant colonies.

Settling additional planets should also become progressively more expensive through colony infrastructure, population support, terraforming, supply, development time, and increasing logistical strain. Habitable planets are common, but each new settlement should still be a meaningful commitment.

Conquered systems may initially be more burdensome than peacefully settled or allied territory because of:

- Damaged infrastructure.
- Occupation inefficiency.
- Temporary stability penalties.
- Increased garrison requirements.
- Diplomatic backlash and local sabotage risk.

Once integrated, a conquered system can become a normal productive part of the empire. The purpose of these costs is to make the player weigh full control against the short-term military and political commitment, not to make conquest permanently inferior.

## Diplomacy as Optimization

Diplomatic discoveries should produce shortcuts and alternative plans:

- Learn that a faction already controls uranium and trade instead of conquering the region.
- Discover a friendly species that can hold a flank.
- Identify a compromised ally before investing in its defense.
- Find the exact resource that makes a treaty possible.
- Use language research to turn a dangerous contact into a coalition partner.

The player should be able to choose among military, economic, and diplomatic solutions to important problems.

## Multiplayer Diplomacy

Player empires should be allies by default but retain meaningful ownership and negotiation.

They can:

- Trade resources.
- Share fleet access.
- Coordinate defense.
- Loan ships or construction capacity.
- Share research or discoveries.
- Build joint projects.
- Agree on who controls a scarce location.

The design should avoid hidden player-versus-player betrayal in the cooperative campaign unless explicitly added as an optional mode. Conflict between players should primarily be about priorities and scarce opportunities, not sabotage of friends.

## Open Questions

- How many active factions can the map support before diplomacy becomes noisy?
- Which procedural constraints ensure faction motives and relationships remain readable and fair?
- Can players transfer territory, or only access and resources?
- How are treaties represented and enforced?
- Can diplomacy redirect or delay the invasion?
- What makes a faction permanently trustworthy?
- How are language and cultural technologies presented to the player?
